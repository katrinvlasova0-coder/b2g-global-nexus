Knowing how to **read a tender notice** correctly is the first technical skill any company bidding for public contracts needs to master. A tender notice is a structured document, and each field — from the CPV code to the submission deadline — carries legal weight. Misreading even one line, such as the exact closing time or which lots are open for combined bids, can disqualify an otherwise strong offer before evaluation even begins.

This explainer breaks down the core sections of a typical tender notice: the contracting authority details, CPV codes and lot structure, deadlines and time zones, eligibility and selection criteria, and award methodology. It draws on public reference sources such as TED (https://ted.europa.eu), the OECD's procurement guidance (https://www.oecd.org/governance/procurement/), and the UNCITRAL Model Law on Public Procurement (https://uncitral.un.org). The goal is to help you build a repeatable checklist before you commit resources to a bid.

## What is a tender notice and why the format matters

A tender notice is the official publication that announces a public procurement opportunity. It is issued by a contracting authority — a government ministry, municipality, state-owned enterprise, or a multilateral-funded project implementing unit — and it sets out the legal and procedural framework for the entire competition.

Most jurisdictions follow a broadly similar structure, influenced by frameworks like the UNCITRAL Model Law and, within the EU, the standard forms used on TED. Even when formats differ between an EU member state's e-procurement portal and, say, a World Bank-financed project notice (https://www.worldbank.org/en/projects-operations/products-and-services/brief/procurement-debarment), the same core fields tend to appear:

- Contracting authority identity and contact point
- Procedure type (open, restricted, negotiated, framework agreement)
- CPV codes or equivalent classification
- Lot division, if any
- Estimated value and duration
- Eligibility and selection criteria
- Submission deadline and opening session details
- Award criteria and weighting

Reading the notice means confirming each of these fields matches your company's capacity and intent before you invest time preparing full documentation, which is covered in more depth in [How to prepare tender documentation](/blog/how-to-prepare-tender-documentation/).

## How CPV codes and tender lots define what you're bidding on

CPV (Common Procurement Vocabulary) codes are an eight-digit classification system used across EU procurement and referenced by many national systems influenced by EU practice. Each code identifies a category of goods, works, or services — for example, construction work, IT consulting, or medical equipment. The CPV code is usually one of the first data points listed in a notice, and it should match the scope described in the technical specifications.

Tender lots split a larger contract into smaller, separately awardable parts. A road-rehabilitation contract might be divided into three geographic lots; an IT framework might separate hardware supply from maintenance services. When reading a notice, check:

1. Whether the contract has lots at all, or is a single indivisible award
2. How many lots exist and what each one covers
3. Whether you may bid for one lot, multiple lots, or must bid for all lots
4. Whether there is a cap on the number of lots a single bidder can win
5. Whether lots have different deadlines, values, or eligibility thresholds

Getting the lot structure wrong is a common early-stage error — companies sometimes prepare a full-scope bid when the authority only wanted separate, smaller submissions per lot, or vice versa.

## How to read deadlines, eligibility, and selection criteria

Deadlines in tender notices are rarely flexible. The notice will state a submission date and time, and increasingly a specific time zone — this matters when the contracting authority and the bidder operate in different regions. Late submissions are typically rejected outright, regardless of technical merit, so this line deserves your first read, not your last.

Eligibility criteria (sometimes called selection criteria) determine whether your company qualifies to participate at all. These commonly include:

| Criterion type | Typical requirement | Where to verify |
|---|---|---|
| Legal standing | Valid business registration, no debarment | National company registry, World Bank debarment list |
| Financial capacity | Minimum annual turnover, liquidity ratio | Audited financial statements |
| Technical capacity | Similar past contracts, certified staff | Reference letters, CVs |
| Quality/management systems | ISO 9001 or equivalent certification | ISO.org and certifying body |
| Bid security | Bid bond or tender guarantee | Bank or insurer confirmation |

Award criteria are separate from eligibility. Once a bidder is deemed eligible, the award stage decides who wins based on published weighting — commonly a mix of price and technical quality, or in some procedures, price alone (lowest evaluated bid). Reading the weighting table tells you where to invest effort: a notice weighted 70% technical / 30% price rewards depth of methodology far more than an aggressive discount.

## Common mistakes when reading a tender notice

Even experienced bidders misread notices under time pressure. The most frequent errors include:

1. Confusing the estimated contract value with the minimum bid security amount
2. Missing a mandatory clarification-question deadline that precedes the submission deadline
3. Assuming national holidays or weekends extend a stated deadline without checking the notice's own rules
4. Overlooking a requirement for consortium or subcontracting declarations
5. Not checking whether amendments or corrigenda were published after the original notice

Corrigenda are especially easy to miss. Authorities frequently publish amendments — extending deadlines, correcting CPV codes, or revising eligibility thresholds — after the initial notice. Always check the notice's version history on platforms like TED before finalizing anything.

## Risks and limits of relying on the notice alone

A tender notice is a summary, not the full legal basis of the procurement. Several limits apply:

- The notice itself is usually shorter than the full tender dossier; detailed technical specifications, draft contracts, and evaluation grids are typically found in separate downloadable files referenced by the notice.
- Eligibility thresholds stated in the notice can be restated with more precision (or additional conditions) inside the qualification documents — always cross-check both.
- Bid or performance security requirements mentioned briefly in the notice often have detailed rules — format, validity period, issuing bank conditions — spelled out elsewhere. See [Bid bonds and tender guarantees explained](/blog/bid-bonds-and-tender-guarantees/) for how these instruments typically work; no bond or guarantee should be treated as automatically available or approved.
- Meeting the published criteria does not guarantee shortlisting or award; evaluation outcomes depend on the full submitted file, the competition from other bidders, and the contracting authority's own internal review, none of which a notice can predict.
- If you plan to rely on subcontractors to meet technical capacity thresholds, the notice's rules on subcontracting disclosure must be followed precisely — see [How to select subcontractors for public works](/blog/how-to-select-subcontractors-public-works/).

Because outcomes depend on local law, the specific evaluation committee, and the completeness of your own file, no article — including this one — can promise a result. Treat the notice as your starting map, not your final answer.

## Practical checklist before you decide to bid

Use this sequence each time you open a new notice:

1. Confirm the contracting authority, procedure type, and reference number
2. Match the CPV code(s) to your registered business activity
3. Identify the lot structure and decide which lot(s) fit your capacity
4. Record the submission deadline with its exact time zone, plus any clarification-question deadline
5. List every eligibility/selection criterion and check your company's supporting evidence for each
6. Note the award criteria weighting (price vs. quality) to plan resource allocation
7. Check for published corrigenda or amendments before finalizing your file
8. Confirm bid security requirements (amount, format, validity) if applicable
9. Verify whether subcontracting or consortium bids are permitted and how they must be disclosed
10. Cross-reference the notice summary against the full tender dossier for consistency

Running through this list against a compliance checklist, such as the one in [Bid compliance checklist](/blog/bid-compliance-checklist/), reduces the risk of a technical rejection before your bid is even evaluated on merit.

## FAQ

**How do I read a tender notice correctly?**
Check the contracting authority and reference number, CPV codes, lot structure, submission deadline and time zone, eligibility and selection criteria, and award criteria, then verify these against the full tender documents.

**What is a CPV code and why does it matter?**
A CPV code is a standardized classification for goods, works, or services in procurement, mainly used across the EU, helping you confirm a contract matches your registered activity.

**What does "lot" mean in a tender notice?**
A lot is a separately awardable portion of a larger contract; notices specify whether you can bid on one, several, or all lots.

**What is the difference between eligibility criteria and award criteria?**
Eligibility criteria determine whether you qualify to bid; award criteria determine how the winner is chosen among qualified bidders, usually based on price, quality, or both.

**Where can I find official tender notices?**
Official notices appear on platforms such as TED, national e-procurement portals, and development bank procurement pages; always verify on the issuing authority's own platform.
