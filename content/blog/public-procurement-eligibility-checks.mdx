## Public procurement eligibility checks: exclusion, capacity, references

Procurement eligibility is the gate a bidder must pass through before a contracting authority even looks at price or technical quality. It is normally assessed in three layers: exclusion grounds (legal and integrity checks), economic and financial capacity (turnover, solvency, insurance), and technical or professional capacity, most often demonstrated through past performance references. If any mandatory eligibility criterion is not met, or not evidenced correctly, the bid can be rejected before the evaluation committee scores anything else.

This explainer walks through how these three layers typically work across EU-regulated tenders, World Bank–financed projects, and UNCITRAL Model Law–based national systems, and what documentation is generally requested to prove each one. It is written for companies that regularly submit public tenders and want a repeatable internal process for eligibility screening, not a one-off checklist for a single bid.

## What "procurement eligibility" actually covers

In most legal frameworks, procurement eligibility is not a single test — it is a bundle of separate checks defined by the contracting authority in the tender notice and the procurement documents. The structure is broadly consistent across jurisdictions that follow EU directives, UNCITRAL's Model Law on Public Procurement, or multilateral development bank procurement frameworks such as the World Bank's.

The three recurring layers are:

1. **Exclusion grounds** — legal and integrity-based reasons to reject a bidder outright, independent of technical merit.
2. **Economic and financial capacity (economic capacity)** — evidence the bidder can financially sustain contract execution.
3. **Technical and professional capacity**, including **past performance** — evidence the bidder has relevant delivery experience.

A fourth, softer layer sometimes appears: professional standing, such as registration in a trade or business register, or possession of specific licenses (e.g., construction permits, medical device authorizations). This is often bundled into eligibility rather than treated as a separate criterion.

Because these criteria are set out per-procedure — not as a universal standard — the first practical step for any bidder is always the same: read the specific notice and documents, not a generic template. ## Exclusion grounds: what disqualifies a bidder

Exclusion grounds are typically split into mandatory and discretionary categories.

**Mandatory exclusion grounds** commonly include:

- Final conviction for participation in a criminal organization, corruption, fraud, terrorist offences, money laundering, or child labor/human trafficking.
- Non-payment of taxes or social security contributions, where established by a final and binding decision.
- Certain sanctions-list matches under applicable national or international sanctions regimes.

**Discretionary exclusion grounds** — which the contracting authority may or may not apply, depending on the legal framework — often include:

- Breach of environmental, social, or labor law obligations.
- Bankruptcy, insolvency, or winding-up proceedings, unless the applicable law allows continued trading.
- Grave professional misconduct that puts the bidder's integrity in doubt.
- Significant or persistent deficiencies in a prior public contract that led to early termination, damages, or comparable sanctions.
- Conflicts of interest that cannot be effectively remedied.

Under EU rules, these grounds broadly follow Directive 2014/24/EU, articles 57–58, and are self-declared initially via the European Single Procurement Document (ESPD) before being verified for the winning bidder. The World Bank applies its own framework, with a public list of debarred and cross-debarred firms and individuals maintained on its procurement pages. UNCITRAL's Model Law leaves member states to define exclusion grounds in national legislation, so wording and thresholds vary by country.

## Economic capacity: proving financial sustainability

Economic and financial capacity checks exist to reduce the risk that a winning bidder cannot actually finance contract execution — payroll, materials, subcontractors, and working capital — until the first milestone payment arrives.

Typical evidence requested includes:

| Capacity indicator | Common documentary evidence | Typical reference period |
|---|---|---|
| Minimum annual turnover | Audited financial statements, tax filings | Last 2–3 financial years |
| Specific-activity turnover | Segmented revenue statement or auditor letter | Last 2–3 financial years |
| Liquidity/solvency ratio | Balance sheet extracts, bank statements | Most recent closed year |
| Professional indemnity insurance | Insurance policy certificate | Valid at submission/award |
| Bank capacity or credit line | Bank reference letter | Issued within submission window |

Thresholds are set by the contracting authority relative to the estimated contract value — commonly expressed as a multiple of the annual contract value, though the exact ratio and lookback period differ by authority and by country. Consortia or joint ventures are usually allowed to combine the capacity of their members, subject to rules on minimum individual shares, which is why many mid-sized firms bid as partners on larger lots rather than alone.

## Past performance: what reference contracts need to show

Technical and professional capacity is most often demonstrated through past performance references — a list of similar contracts completed within a defined window, usually the last three to five years, depending on the sector and the contracting authority's rules.

A credible reference package typically includes:

1. A structured list of similar contracts (client name, contract value, dates, scope description).
2. Completion or satisfaction certificates issued by the client, ideally on letterhead with a signature and date.
3. Evidence that contract value and scope match or exceed the minimum thresholds stated in the tender documents.
4. Proof of similarity — same CPV category, comparable technical complexity, or comparable geographic/regulatory context, where required.
5. Named key personnel with CVs and qualifications, when the notice requires evidence of team-level experience rather than only corporate experience.

Contracting authorities generally define "similar" narrowly — a road resurfacing reference will rarely satisfy a bridge construction requirement, even within the same CPV division. Reading the minimum-requirement wording literally, rather than assuming a broad reading will be accepted, is one of the most common gaps in eligibility submissions.

## How eligibility checks fit into the overall evaluation

Eligibility screening (sometimes called the "qualification" or "selection" stage) is usually separated from technical and financial evaluation. The sequence generally looks like this:

1. **Formal/administrative compliance check** — is the submission complete, signed, and within deadline?
2. **Exclusion ground screening** — self-declarations, and for the leading bidder, supporting documents or database checks.
3. **Economic and technical capacity screening** — turnover, solvency, and reference contracts against the stated minimums.
4. **Technical evaluation** — scoring of the actual proposal against award criteria.
5. **Financial evaluation** — price or price-quality ratio scoring.

Bids that fail steps 1–3 are typically excluded before steps 4–5 occur, regardless of price competitiveness. This is why eligibility documentation deserves the same rigor as the technical proposal. ## Risks and limits: what eligibility checks cannot guarantee

It is worth being direct about the limits of this process. Passing eligibility checks does not guarantee contract award — it only opens the door to technical and financial evaluation, where the bid still competes on merit against published award criteria. There is no guaranteed financing, no guaranteed bank guarantee issuance, and no guaranteed outcome tied to any documentation package, however complete.

Common risk points include:

- **Self-declaration mismatches.** What a company declares in an ESPD or equivalent self-declaration must match what supporting documents later show; discrepancies can trigger exclusion even after a bid otherwise scores well.
- **Reference contract disputes.** A client's failure to confirm a reference contract's terms in writing can invalidate an otherwise strong past-performance claim.
- **Consortium capacity aggregation limits.** Some frameworks cap how much a joint venture can rely on a minority partner's turnover or references.
- **Sanctions and debarment list drift.** Sanctions lists and debarment registers change; a check done months before submission may be outdated by the deadline.
- **Jurisdictional variation.** Thresholds, lookback periods, and even the definition of "grave professional misconduct" differ meaningfully between EU member states, World Bank–financed projects, and UNCITRAL-based national systems — templates from one jurisdiction rarely transfer cleanly to another.

Because of this variability, eligibility screening should be treated as a recurring internal discipline, tied to each specific notice, rather than a static compliance file reused across unrelated tenders.

## A practical eligibility screening checklist

Before assembling a full bid, most experienced bidders run a short internal eligibility pass. A workable version looks like this:

1. Confirm the notice's exclusion-ground wording and cross-check against current sanctions/debarment lists.
2. Verify turnover and solvency figures against the stated minimum thresholds and reference period.
3. Confirm insurance coverage is valid through the award and, where applicable, execution period.
4. Pull at least the minimum number of qualifying reference contracts and confirm each has an available certificate or contactable referee.
5. Check consortium/subcontracting rules if capacity or references will be combined across entities.
6. Diarize sanctions-list and company-register rechecks close to the submission deadline, not only at kickoff.

Building this into a repeatable internal process — rather than recreating it for every tender — is one of the more durable investments a contracting-focused company can make, especially when bidding across [multiple jurisdictions](/blog/how-to-find-public-tenders-worldwide/) with different eligibility frameworks.
